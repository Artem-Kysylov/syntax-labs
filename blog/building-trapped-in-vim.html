Building Trapped in Vim: How an AI-Designed Boss Took Me 38 Attempts to Beat

By Artem Kysylov

Every Vim tutorial eventually tells you the same thing: stop reaching for the arrow keys. Mine apparently didn't stop at tutorials. A few weeks after HJKL finally stuck in my fingers, I decided the only reasonable next step was to build an entire 8-bit platformer where arrow keys don't exist at all. The result is Trapped in Vim — a browser game that punishes you for forgetting your home row, and occasionally punishes me for shipping it.

Why a Platformer, Why Vim Keys

The premise is simple and slightly mean: you're a pixel trapped inside a terminal, and the only way out is H (left), J (down), K (up), and L (right). No WASD, no arrow key fallback, no mercy. If you've never touched Vim, the first level is a crash course disguised as a dungeon. If you have, it's muscle memory doing the work your brain refuses to.

I wanted something I could actually finish — not a sprawling engine, just a focused 8-bit experience built on an HTML canvas, vanilla JavaScript, and a controller scheme that forces the "aha" moment Vim beginners eventually get. Turns out making players relearn how to move is a great way to make a tutorial feel like a game instead of a chore.

"Every other platformer trusts you with four directional keys. This one makes you earn them."

Three Levels and One Room I Didn't Tell Anyone About

The game ships with three levels, each one layering on a new Vim-flavored obstacle — timing jumps on K, dodging hazards that only open to a precise L-tap, that kind of thing. There's also a secret room, hidden well enough that most playtesters walked straight past it without noticing the wall wasn't really a wall. I'm not going to spell out where it is here — that would defeat the entire point of a secret room — but if you've ever accidentally discovered a Vim shortcut you didn't know existed, you already have the right instinct for finding it.

A Soundtrack Nobody Asked For

Somewhere around level two I decided the game needed music, and more specifically that it needed music I wrote myself instead of a royalty-free loop. So I built a small chiptune engine directly on top of the Web Audio API — square waves, a noise channel for percussion, and just enough arpeggiation to sound convincingly retro without licensing a single sample. No audio files ship with the game; every note is generated at runtime, which also means the entire soundtrack weighs almost nothing on the page.

It was, objectively, more work than the feature deserved. It was also the most fun I had building this thing.

MONITOR KERNEL: The Boss an AI Designed to Hurt Me

The boss fight at the end of level two is called MONITOR KERNEL, and I'll be honest about its origin: I prompted an AI for boss-fight pattern ideas, expecting something tame. What came back was a multi-phase attack rotation with enough deliberate timing windows to feel fair in theory and brutal in practice. I implemented it faithfully, mostly because I was too proud to nerf a boss I didn't even design.

That decision cost me 38 attempts to beat my own boss fight. I kept a tally. It felt important to keep a tally.

In hindsight, that's the right outcome. A boss fight that the developer breezes through on attempt two is a boss fight that will get complained about in the reviews. MONITOR KERNEL earned its name honestly — it's unforgiving, it's readable once you learn its tells, and it made me genuinely relieved the first time I walked away from it still alive.

Open Source, Pull Requests Welcome

The full source is public on GitHub. If you want to poke around the rendering loop, the Web Audio synth, or the input handling that turns HJKL into platformer movement, it's all there to read. And if you've got an idea for a fourth level, a new mechanic, or a boss meaner than MONITOR KERNEL — pull requests are genuinely welcome. I built this solo, but I'd rather it not stay that way.

Play it, break it, find the secret room, and if you manage to beat the boss in fewer than 38 tries, I'd like to know how.
